# People in their light

Apply the shared philosophy when a face, gesture, or human relationship carries the photograph. These are practical applications of the shared principles, not a newly researched account of portrait masters.

Read the encounter before correcting skin. A shaded face beside a bright window can become more legible while remaining shaded. A performer under saturated stage light should still belong to that light. Tinted glasses, reflected foliage, and coloured surroundings can explain local colour; a familiar skin tone is not a universal neutral reference. Judge whether the colour describes the illumination or merely makes the face difficult to read.

Choose what recognition requires. An eye or expression may need clearer separation, but lifting hair, face, clothing, and background together can erase intimacy. Judge the face alongside the neck, hands, and nearby people. A face that seems independently lit, or a conspicuous oval of brightness around it, signals that the emphasis has become visible as an intervention.

A silhouette can remain convincing with a little readable clothing, ground or environmental context. When the lower frame feels empty, judge whether faint information would anchor the figure without weakening the sky and rim light. Make the requested improvement perceptible at normal viewing size, then assess the noise and colour exposed in those shadows. Complete blackness and uniform shadow visibility are both choices, not requirements.

In a group, protect relationships among people. Keep comparable illumination coherent without forcing different complexions to identical brightness or colour. Do not privilege the easiest face to edit while leaving others distractingly dark. Background and table light may explain the gathering; reducing their dominance should not remove the room from the encounter.

Separate skin texture, makeup, optical softness, motion, and noise. Cleaning colour noise can improve presence; smoothing every variation can erase it. A soft source can support an expressive portrait without supporting a claim of restored eyelashes. Judge whether the finished viewing size serves the expression and whether any increased brightness makes the original limitations more intrusive.
