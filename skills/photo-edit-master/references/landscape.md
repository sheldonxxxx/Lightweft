# Landscape judgment

Use with the shared philosophy in `SKILL.md` when place, space, weather, land, or natural pattern carries the photograph. These are applications of our synthesis, not separate looks to assign to scenes.

## Find what makes this place specific

The subject may be expansive space, interwoven growth, repeated geology, weather, reflected colour, human presence, or change over time. A mountain photograph can concern distance or human use more than its summit. Choose the relationship with the strongest visible support and let it determine what deserves emphasis.

Grand scale needs internal relationships: masses, intervals, routes of attention, and proportion. Drama without them becomes spectacle without structure. Intimate landscapes may depend on distributed attention: distinguish enough for the eye to discover order without reducing interdependence and abundance to one protagonist. A surface pattern or abstraction need not imitate the depth of an expansive view.

## Let atmosphere and material explain the space

When depth matters, preserve believable differences between near and far and continuity of atmosphere. Stronger separation helps when it reveals essential form; it harms when distant ridges acquire the insistence of nearby rocks. Foreground texture should contribute to the whole rather than dominate simply because it contains abundant detail.

Fog, spray, rain, dust, and snow can explain distance, movement, and conditions. Concealment becomes expressive when its cause is legible. Do not automatically clear it away or romanticise it: judge whether it supports the particular encounter or leaves what matters inaccessible.

Snow can carry blue shade and water reflected colour. A small warm accent may depend on the cooler field surrounding it; a broad quiet colour may unify the scene. Preserve those relationships rather than imposing a habitual nature palette. Luminosity can reside in pale areas without every dark region becoming equally descriptive. Decide whether colour is essential before exchanging it for monochrome form and rhythm.

## Treat the frame as an interpretation of place

More space may preserve scale or a route through the landscape; less may reveal a concentrated relationship. Judge the exchange. A road can be evidence of habitation, and damaged ground can be central to the photograph. A bare hillside is observable; its cause may require evidence outside the frame.

Do not equate an ideal landscape with pristine wilderness, golden light, saturated colour, or an empty human world. Ordinary daylight, dormant vegetation, inhabited places, and difficult facts can sustain serious photographs. Beauty, unease, exuberance, quiet, and contradiction are possible responses when supported by what is visible.

## Turn the reading into a bounded decision

For a hypothetical misty valley, an intention might be to reveal the rhythm of overlapping land while preserving the sensation of distance. Name the relevant layers and competing regions in the actual image; let their distinction improve while the far ridge still recedes. Equal crispness across all layers would defeat that intention. If the frame changes the balance of foreground and distance, reassess their relative emphasis together.

This example demonstrates a relationship, a desired gain, and a sign of excess. A woodland whose meaning lies in dense interdependence may need a different decision. Choose from this photograph's evidence, not from the scene label.
