# Choosing a film emulation

Use film colour and tone to support a particular photograph. Choose from the editor's installed inventory, preserving its exact names. The six names below are RapidRAW's built-in Spektrafilm looks; similarly named presets in other editors can behave differently.

## Evidence and scope

The rendering observations below come from matched native RAW comparisons of an autumn landscape and a portrait under mixed light. Each used a common exposure-adjusted base, unchanged framing, all six looks at full strength, and a half-strength Portra alternative. Ektar produced the largest colour change in both samples; Portra was gentler, and its reduced-strength version retained more of the base. Native detail showed existing portrait noise becoming more conspicuous under stronger colour and contrast. These observations establish those samples, not a universal ranking or physical film accuracy.

The suggested uses are artistic inferences from those renders and the manufacturer background below. Daylight portraits, snow, fog, neon, wildlife and night skies still need their own comparisons. Reproduce a comparison in the available editor with a retained base, equal initial exposure and white balance, one look changed at a time, and matching overview and relevant native detail. Once the look is understood, exposure or white balance may be adapted deliberately and labelled as part of the candidate.

## How the agent should suggest a film

Read the photograph first: subject relationship, illumination, dominant colours, saturation headroom, important shadows and highlights, source noise, and the user's desired mood. Then decide whether film interpretation offers a specific gain. Respect a requested neutral or colour-faithful result; retaining the base is a valid recommendation, particularly for exact product colour, identification details or delicate night-sky colour.

Choose a leading available candidate and, only if useful, a contrasting alternative. Name the visible reason, intended change and likely cost. For example: “The amber leaves frame a quieter grey lake. Gold could connect their warmth; I would compare it with the base and watch that the far bank still recedes.” For a shaded face: “A restrained Portra treatment could enrich the warm light, but the dark hair needs to stay readable.” These are reasoning examples, not instructions to use those films on every similar subject.

Distinguish **suggested**, **rendered and inspected**, and **user accepted**. An analysis request needs a recommendation, not an automatic edit. Within an authorized editing brief, render a reversible candidate and keep the base. Do not save a personal preference or reusable preset solely because the agent prefers it.

Use strength as a blend decision: test enough of the look to understand its direction, then reduce it if the intended relationship survives with fewer costs. The half-strength Portra sample is an example, not a default recipe. Do not copy the study's exposure adjustment into new photographs. Judge the full frame, skin and important neutrals, saturated colours, shadow legibility, and native detail where noise or texture changes. Retain the look only when the expressive gain is visible and worth its cost.

Publish the recommendation alongside the candidate in the shared review surface: why this film here, what to preserve, and the sign that it has gone too far. Record the editor, actual LUT identity, strength, base, changed adjustments and inspected regions in workspace provenance. Update later recommendations from the user's saved feedback; keep personal preferences separate from this general guide.

## Film names describe an influence, not new capture properties

A colour LUT does not change sensor ISO, create recoverable highlight detail, improve low-light sensitivity, remove noise, or reproduce the full physical film/print/scan process. Stock numbers are not instructions to choose an emulation matching the camera's ISO. Grain, halation and optical softness require separate supported effects and their own visual judgment. Check the editor's expected colour pipeline before interpreting a surprising result; a wrong LUT input space can overwhelm any artistic choice.

## Manufacturer and model background

- [Fujifilm PRO 400H brochure](https://asset.fujifilm.com/www/us/files/2020-03/14c3c3e650b41069b071bfd3180bd936/FUJICOLOR_PRO_NEG_FAMILY_BROCHURE_101305.pdf) describes portrait, wedding and commercial uses, skin reproduction and colour consistency across varied illumination. This does not establish a pastel rendering for every emulation.
- [Kodak Portra 400 technical data](https://business.kodakmoments.com/sites/default/files/files/resources/e4050_portra_400.pdf) emphasizes skin reproduction and portrait, fashion and travel uses across changing light. The gentler rendering recommendation above also relies on the sample comparisons.
- [Kodak Ektar 100 technical data](https://business.kodakmoments.com/sites/default/files/files/resources/e4046_ektar_100.pdf) describes strong saturation and nature, travel, outdoor, fashion and product applications. The physical stock's fine grain is not a noise-reduction promise for the LUT.
- [Kodak Gold 200](https://business.kodakmoments.com/product/kodak-gold-200-film) describes colourful everyday photographs in daylight or flash. The orange/warm interpretation above is a sample observation and an artistic use, not a guaranteed manufacturer colour cast.
- [Kodak VISION3 250D](https://www.kodak.com/en/motion/product/camera-films/250d-5207-7207/) and [500T](https://www.kodak.com/en/motion/product/camera-films/500t-5219-7219/) provide the daylight and tungsten/low-light stock context. Their capture latitude and grain claims belong to physical film, not to these fixed digital looks.
- [Spektrafilm](https://github.com/andreavolpato/spektrafilm) models a negative, print and scan pipeline and can bake colour transforms into LUTs. A built-in LUT is a particular rendering choice from that larger model; the editor's implementation and current rendered pixels remain decisive.
